02Cosmic Neighbourhood

The Sun — our nearest star

  • A giant ball of glowing hot gases — mainly hydrogen and helium — that gives out its own light and heat.
  • It is about 150 million km away from Earth — the light you see left it about 8 minutes ago.
  • Over 13 lakh Earths could fit inside the Sun. It is the centre of our solar system.
  • All life on Earth depends on its energy — for warmth, light, food and weather.
FUN FACT The Sun makes up about 99.8% of all the mass in our solar system — Earth and everything else is just the leftover 0.2%!
03Motion of the Earth

Rotation — day and night

  • Earth spins on its axis from west to east — this is called rotation.
  • One rotation takes about 24 hours. The side facing the Sun has day; the other side has night.
  • This is why the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west — it is really we who are moving.
FUN FACT At the equator you are spinning around at about 1,670 km/h right now — you can't feel it because everything around you moves along with you!
04Motion of the Earth

Revolution — the changing night sky

  • Earth also moves around the Sun in a fixed path called its orbit. This is revolution.
  • One revolution takes 365¼ days — one year. Every 4 years, the extra ¼ days add up to make a leap year.
  • As we journey around the Sun, we face different stars at night in different seasons — so the night sky slowly changes through the year.
  • Constellations like Orion and Ursa Major appear at different times of the year.
FUN FACT Earth races around the Sun at about 107,000 km/h — fast enough to circle our whole planet in just one minute!
05Seasons on the Earth

Why do seasons happen?

  • Earth's axis is tilted at 23.5° — and it always points the same way as we orbit.
  • In June, the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun — sunlight is more concentrated and days are longer. It's summer!
  • In December, it leans away — rays spread out, days are shorter. Winter! Seasons are opposite in the Southern Hemisphere.
FUN FACT Seasons have nothing to do with our distance from the Sun — Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January, during the Northern Hemisphere's winter!
06Our Companion

The Moon and its phases

  • The Moon is Earth's natural satellite. It has no light of its own — it only reflects sunlight.
  • It revolves around Earth in about 27 days, and rotates at the same rate — so we always see the same face.
  • As the Moon circles us, we see different lit portions — New Moon → Crescent → First Quarter → Full Moon → and back.
  • The full cycle of phases takes about 29½ days — roughly one month, or a lunar month.
FUN FACT The Moon is slowly drifting away from Earth — about 3.8 cm every year, roughly the speed your fingernails grow!
07Eclipses

The solar eclipse

  • An eclipse happens when the Earth, Moon and Sun line up in a straight line.
  • In a solar eclipse, the Moon comes between the Sun and Earth, casting its shadow on us. Day briefly turns dark.
  • It can only happen on a new moon day, and is visible only from parts of Earth inside the Moon's shadow.
FUN FACT By amazing coincidence, the Sun is about 400 times bigger than the Moon but also 400 times farther away — so they look the same size in our sky, making perfect eclipses possible!
08Eclipses

The lunar eclipse

  • In a lunar eclipse, the Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon — the Moon passes into Earth's shadow.
  • The full Moon slowly darkens, and can even look dull reddish-brown ("blood moon"), because some sunlight bends through Earth's atmosphere.
  • It happens only on a full moon night and is safe to watch with the naked eye.
  • A lunar eclipse is visible from the entire night side of Earth at once.
FUN FACT During a total lunar eclipse, the Moon can glow a coppery red — often called a "Blood Moon" — as sunlight bends through Earth's atmosphere onto it.
09Think Deeper

Why no eclipse every month?

  • If the Moon's orbit lay flat in the same plane as Earth's orbit, we would see an eclipse every month.
  • But the Moon's path is tilted about 5° — so most months the shadows miss each other.
  • Eclipses occur only when the line-up happens near the two crossing points, called nodes — roughly 4–7 times a year worldwide.
FUN FACT Ancient Babylonians predicted eclipses thousands of years ago using an 18-year, 11-day pattern called the "Saros cycle"!
10Fascinating Facts

Reading the night sky

  • Groups of stars forming patterns are called constellations — like Saptarshi (Ursa Major), the Great Bear with seven bright stars.
  • The Pole Star (Dhruva Tara) stays fixed in the north and has guided travellers for thousands of years.
  • Fascinating fact: In India, the Bhil and Pawara tribes of Maharashtra use the position of stars to mark the arrival of the monsoon rains.
  • Stars are huge, far-away suns — they only seem tiny and twinkling because of their great distance.
FUN FACT Light from the nearest star after our Sun — Proxima Centauri — takes over 4 years to reach Earth. We see it as 4 years ago!
11Be a Safe Scientist

Watching an eclipse safely

  • Never look at the Sun directly — not with naked eyes, sunglasses, X-ray films or cameras. It can permanently damage your eyes.
  • Safe methods: pinhole projection, specially made eclipse glasses (certified filters), or welding glass of shade 14.
  • A lunar eclipse is completely safe to watch directly — no filters needed!
FUN FACT Pinhole viewers were used by ancient astronomers over 2,000 years ago — a simple idea older than the telescope itself!
